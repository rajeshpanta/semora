"Weighted GPA" gets used two different ways, and it's worth separating them before doing any math. This guide covers the one that matters week to week in an individual course: calculating your current grade from weighted categories. (The other common meaning — a school-wide GPA scale that adds extra points for honors or AP courses — is a separate, institution-specific policy and isn't what's covered here.) Further down, we'll also untangle a third, related source of confusion: the difference between a single course's grade, your GPA for a semester, and your cumulative GPA — three different numbers that all get called "GPA" at some point.

## The formula

A weighted course grade is calculated as:

**Weighted grade = (sum of each category's score times its weight) divided by (sum of the weights that have graded work so far)**

The key detail: a category only counts once it actually has graded work in it. A final exam worth 30% of your grade doesn't drag your current average down to zero before you've taken it — it simply isn't part of the calculation yet.

## Worked example 1: a simple four-category course

Say a course is weighted like this: Homework 20%, Midterm 30%, Final 30%, Participation 20%.

Partway through the semester, only homework and the midterm have been graded:

- Homework average: 92%, weight 20
- Midterm: 85%, weight 30

Weighted grade = (92 × 20 + 85 × 30) divided by (20 + 30) = (1,840 + 2,550) divided by 50 = 4,390 divided by 50 = **87.8%**

Participation and the final aren't graded yet, so their 50 combined weight isn't part of the denominator — only the 50 weight that has scores attached to it counts.

## Worked example 2: adding a category mid-semester

Now participation gets graded too: 98%, weight 20.

Weighted grade = (92 × 20 + 85 × 30 + 98 × 20) divided by (20 + 30 + 20) = (1,840 + 2,550 + 1,960) divided by 70 = 6,350 divided by 70 = **90.7%**

Notice the grade moved up even though homework and the midterm didn't change — adding a strong participation score into the mix shifted the weighted average, because the denominator grew along with the numerator.

## Why this differs from a simple average

If you just averaged 92, 85, and 98 without weights, you'd get 91.7% — close to the example above, but not the same, and the gap grows fast in courses where one category is worth much more than another. A final exam worth 30% deserves far more influence over your grade than a single 5%-weighted quiz; a plain average would treat them identically, which is why weighted grading exists in the first place.

## Worked example 3: a category with a "drop the lowest" rule

The first two examples had one thing working in their favor: every score fed directly into its category's average. Real syllabi are often messier — a common wrinkle is a policy like "lowest quiz score dropped," which changes what goes into the calculation, not just the calculation itself.

Say a course is weighted like this: Quizzes 15% (lowest of 6 dropped), Homework 15%, Midterm 1 15%, Midterm 2 20%, Final 35%.

Partway through the semester, all 6 quizzes have been given, homework has a running average, and Midterm 1 has been graded. Midterm 2 and the final are still ahead. The six quiz scores are 88, 95, 72, 90, 85, and 91.

The 72 is the lowest, so it gets dropped before anything else happens — not averaged in and then subtracted out afterward. The remaining five scores are 88, 95, 90, 85, and 91:

Quiz category average = (88 + 95 + 90 + 85 + 91) divided by 5 = 449 divided by 5 = **89.8%**

Homework is averaging 94%, and Midterm 1 came back at 81%. Midterm 2 and the final don't have grades yet, so — same rule as before — their combined 55 weight sits outside the calculation for now:

Weighted grade = (89.8 × 15 + 94 × 15 + 81 × 15) divided by (15 + 15 + 15) = (1,347 + 1,410 + 1,215) divided by 45 = 3,972 divided by 45 = **88.3%**

It's worth seeing what happens if you skip the drop rule and just average all six quizzes: (88 + 95 + 72 + 90 + 85 + 91) divided by 6 = 521 divided by 6 = 86.8%, which is a full three points lower than the 89.8% that comes from dropping the 72 first. Run that lower number through the same weighted formula and the overall grade comes out to roughly 87.3% instead of 88.3% — a one-point difference in the final answer, caused entirely by doing the "drop the lowest" step at the wrong point in the calculation. The rule has to be applied inside the category, before that category's average is computed, not to the finished weighted grade at the end.

## What a borderline grade like 89.5% actually means

At many U.S. schools, the default convention is that anything at or above 89.5% rounds up to 90, which usually lands on the A- cutoff. That's ordinary "round half up" arithmetic applied to a final percentage — but it's a policy choice, not a law of math, and it isn't universal. A few things vary from class to class:

- **Whether rounding happens at all.** Some professors round every final grade to the nearest whole percent before assigning a letter; others take the percentage exactly as calculated, so an 89.49% is simply a B+, no rounding applied, full stop.
- **When rounding happens.** Rounding the single final percentage at the end of the semester can land differently than rounding after every individual assignment throughout the term, since small roundings can compound in either direction over a dozen or more graded items.
- **Where the cutoffs actually sit.** Plenty of syllabi do use 90/80/70/60 as the A/B/C/D lines, but not all of them — some set the A- cutoff at 93%, or use uneven gaps between letter grades rather than a uniform 10 (or 3) percentage points.

None of that is answerable from the arithmetic alone, which is exactly why it's worth reading your specific syllabus's stated grading policy — or emailing to ask directly — rather than assuming a standard rounding rule will turn an 89.4% into a 90%.

## Course grade, semester GPA, and cumulative GPA are three different numbers

Everything calculated above is one specific thing: the current weighted grade in a single course, expressed as a percentage. That's genuinely useful on its own, but it's only one of three numbers that regularly get called "GPA," and mixing them up causes real confusion.

1. **A single course's weighted grade.** The percentage from the examples above (87.8%, 90.7%, 88.3%). Strictly speaking this isn't "GPA" at all — it's a course grade — but people often use the terms loosely, especially mid-semester before any letter grade has been assigned.
2. **Semester GPA.** This starts from each course's final letter grade for the semester, converted to a point value on a 4.0 scale. The standard conversion at most U.S. schools is A = 4.0, A- = 3.7, B+ = 3.3, B = 3.0, B- = 2.7, C+ = 2.3, C = 2.0, C- = 1.7, D+ = 1.3, D = 1.0, D- = 0.7, F = 0.0 (some schools use a simpler version without pluses and minuses). Each course's point value gets multiplied by its credit hours, those totals are summed, and the sum is divided by the total credit hours for the semester. A student with a 4-credit A- and a 3-credit B, for example, would have a semester GPA of (3.7 × 4 + 3.0 × 3) divided by (4 + 3) = (14.8 + 9) divided by 7 = 23.8 divided by 7 = **3.4**.
3. **Cumulative GPA.** The same credit-hour-weighted calculation as semester GPA, but run across every course from every semester completed so far, not just the current one — this is the number that ends up on an official transcript.

The rest of this guide focuses on the first of these: the per-course weighted percentage, calculated automatically as scores are entered. ## When the syllabus doesn't spell out the weights clearly

Not every syllabus presents grading categories as a clean table with a percentage next to each one. Some professors describe the grading policy as a paragraph tucked into a "Course Policies" or "Evaluation" section rather than a table, and it's easy to skim past a sentence like "homework will count for a substantial portion of the final grade" without noticing that no actual number was ever given.

When that happens:

- **Don't default to assuming equal weighting.** It's a common guess when nothing else is stated, but it's frequently wrong, and building a tracked grade on top of a wrong assumption means the number drifts further from reality with every new score entered.
- **Email the professor, or check the course's LMS page, before treating a guessed weight as fact.** It's a two-minute question that prevents weeks of an inaccurate running grade.
- **Ask about edge cases at the same time.** Extra credit, whether a dropped assignment changes the denominator, and how late or incomplete work factors in rarely make it into a syllabus's headline weights, but they affect the same calculation.
- **Update your records immediately if weights change mid-semester.** It happens more often than you'd expect — don't keep calculating against numbers a professor has since revised.
