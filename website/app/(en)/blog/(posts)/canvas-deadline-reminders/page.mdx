Canvas is where instructors post assignments, but it isn't built to make sure you notice a deadline before it arrives. Notifications are opt-in per event type, configured in account settings rather than turned on by default, and even with them enabled, Canvas doesn't offer a built-in way to say "remind me three days before this is due" — you get a notification close to (or after) the fact, not a heads-up with enough runway to actually plan around it. Below is what actually causes deadlines to slip through, how to audit the notification settings you already have, and what it takes to close the gap Canvas leaves open.

## Why deadlines slip through

A few reasons this happens more than students expect:

- **Notification preferences are per-category and easy to under-configure.** It's common to have due-date notifications quietly turned off, or set to a digest that arrives once a day rather than in the moment.
- **There's no advance-warning system.** Even a fully configured Canvas notification tends to fire close to the deadline, not days ahead, so there's little room to start something you'd forgotten about.
- **Canvas shows assignments per course, not workload across all of them.** A single course's assignment list looks manageable in isolation; it's only when several courses' deadlines land in the same week that the real crunch becomes visible, and Canvas doesn't surface that view proactively.
- **Grades and deadlines live separately.** Canvas can show you a due date or a grade, but it doesn't combine the two into a running sense of "how much is this assignment actually worth to my grade, and is it worth the time crunch."

## How to actually check your Canvas notification settings

Before assuming Canvas notifications are broken or "just don't work," it's worth spending five minutes confirming what's actually turned on, because the most common failure mode isn't a Canvas bug — it's a setting that was never configured the way a student assumed it was.

Canvas's account notification preferences page typically lets you set a delivery method — immediately, a daily digest, a weekly digest, or never — independently for each notification type, rather than one master switch for the whole platform. That distinction matters: a student can be fully convinced notifications are "on" while one specific category, the one that actually matters most, is quietly set to never or buried in a digest they don't open daily.

The exact wording and layout can vary by institution and change as Canvas updates its interface, so treat the following as a general shape rather than an exact map of what you'll see. The categories that commonly show up in some form include:

- **Announcements** — instructor posts to the whole class
- **Grading** — a score is posted or changed
- **Due Date** — an assignment's due date is added or changed
- **Assignment Changes** — the assignment itself is edited after the fact
- **Late Grades or submission status** — something related to a missed or late submission

## Which categories are worth prioritizing

Because each of these is typically independent, it's worth walking through them one at a time rather than assuming they're all aligned: go to Account, then Notifications, inside Canvas, and look down the list checking whether the categories that matter most to you — usually Due Date, and, if your school relies on it, Assignment Changes — are set to arrive immediately rather than "never" or a digest you don't check every day. A notification that was technically sent but is sitting unread in a weekly digest email is functionally identical to no notification at all.

It's also worth knowing that this settings page is generally a global, account-level setting rather than something configured separately per course. Checking it once tends to apply across every class you're enrolled in, now and later, which makes the five minutes a one-time cost rather than a chore to repeat each semester.

## Notification fatigue: why turning on everything isn't the fix

Once you've found that settings page, the instinct is often to set every category to "immediately" — announcements, grading, due dates, all of it, all the time. That's usually a mistake, and not a subtle one.

Once a phone is buzzing for every quiz score, every syllabus edit, and every discussion-board reply across five or six courses, the sheer volume becomes its own problem: it trains you to treat notifications as background noise rather than signal worth acting on. The one alert that's genuinely time-sensitive — a due date that just moved up by two days — gets the same half-second glance and dismissal as everything else in the stream. This pattern is sometimes called notification fatigue, and it isn't unique to Canvas; it shows up in any system that treats "more alerts" as inherently safer, when past a certain point each additional alert doesn't add safety — it competes with the ones before it for the same sliver of attention, and often loses.

## Why three days out changes a decision and the morning of doesn't

What actually determines whether a reminder is useful isn't how many arrive, it's when the important ones arrive relative to the deadline. A single, well-timed reminder three days out is worth more than ten same-day pings spread across ten different assignments, because three days out is when there's still something to do about it — start the reading, ask a clarifying question over email, rearrange a weekend that was about to fill up with something else. A reminder that lands the morning something is due can only confirm what you already suspected; it can't undo a week of not knowing.

That's the actual argument for reminders that are fewer and better-timed, rather than simply turned up to maximum. Semora's same-day reminders, on by default on every tier, cover the baseline case — don't let today's deadline slip past unnoticed. Pro's custom reminder timing adds the earlier warning that same-day-only systems structurally can't provide: a 1-day or 3-day advance notice, set once and applied automatically rather than something you have to remember to check for manually. Quiet hours, also on Pro, address the other half of the same problem — a reminder that fires at 2 a.m. doesn't help anyone plan a day differently, so quiet hours push it to a point where it's actually actionable instead of just another buzz to dismiss half-asleep and forget by morning.

## What Canvas notifications can't do

Even a perfectly configured Canvas notification setup has a ceiling on what it's able to tell you, because Canvas notifications are built around events — an assignment was posted, a due date was set, a grade was entered — not around judgment calls about what those events actually mean for you.

## No course can see any other course's calendar

Canvas can tell you a paper is due Friday. It can't tell you that this particular paper is worth 30 percent of your final grade in a class where your average currently sits at a B-minus, while a problem set due that same week in a different class is worth 2 percent. Both show up in the notification feed looking about the same — an assignment, a due date — even though one deserves a Saturday and the other really doesn't. Canvas also has no visibility into your other courses at the same time; it won't tell you that Friday's paper, a midterm elsewhere, and a lab report are all landing in the same seven-day window, because no single course's notification feed can see into any other course's calendar.

That's the gap between an assignment feed and an actual workload picture, and it's exactly where a grade-aware tool adds something a due-date notification structurally cannot. Semora's grade tracking, included on the free tier, keeps a running sense of what each assignment is actually worth, so a due-date reminder can sit next to a "this is 30 percent of your grade" fact instead of next to nothing. On Pro, Academic Risk alerts go a step further, flagging the compound situations Canvas notifications have no mechanism to surface on their own — a falling grade, a piece of missing work, or a week where several courses' deadlines have quietly stacked on top of each other — along with a specific recovery-step plan, rather than leaving it to you to notice the pattern by cross-referencing five different Canvas course pages yourself.

## Setting realistic expectations

None of this — Canvas's own notifications, Semora's reminders, or any other system — replaces the basic habit of occasionally opening a calendar and looking a week or two ahead on your own. Reminders are good at catching the specific thing you'd otherwise forget entirely: the paper that slipped your mind because it was assigned in week two and due in week eleven, the office hours that only happen on a day you don't normally have class. They're less good as a complete substitute for keeping your own general sense of where the semester stands, because a reminder only fires for things a system already knows about, on a schedule someone configured in advance — it can't tell you "you're falling behind in this class overall" the way glancing at your own calendar and noticing three assignments clustered together in the same week can.

The realistic version of a good system, then, is reminders plus a habit, not reminders instead of one. Let same-day and advance reminders handle the specific dates so nothing slips through unnoticed, but keep the once-a-week or once-every-two-weeks habit of opening the calendar and looking ahead anyway — not because the reminders are likely to fail, but because a periodic look at the whole picture catches things no single reminder was ever built to catch: a slow buildup of small assignments, a stretch of the semester that's noticeably heavier than the rest, or a deadline worth starting on today even though nothing is telling you to yet.
