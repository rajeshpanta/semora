Finals week rewards planning more than any other stretch of the semester, mostly because the thing that makes it hard — several high-stakes exams landing in the same short window — is entirely visible in advance. The plan below is built around that visibility.

## Step 1: List every final and what it's actually worth

Start with a simple inventory: every final exam, its date and time, and its weight toward your course grade. A final worth 40% of a course you're already struggling in deserves a different amount of attention than one worth 15% of a course where your grade is already solid — treating every final as equally urgent is how time gets misallocated.

## Step 2: Combine weight with your current standing

Weight alone isn't the full picture — combine it with where your grade currently stands in that course. A high-weight final in a course where you're already comfortably ahead needs less defensive studying than a lower-weight final in a course where your grade is borderline. This is where checking your actual current grade in each course, not just a general sense of how it's going, changes the plan.

## Step 3: Map the exam calendar for density, not just dates

Lay out every final on a single calendar and look for clusters — two exams on the same day, or three within 48 hours. A final that would otherwise get a full week of review time might only realistically get two days if it's boxed in by two other exams. Spotting this in advance is what allows you to shift review earlier for the exams caught in a dense cluster, rather than discovering the collision the week it happens.

## Step 4: Schedule review sessions like appointments

Once you know which finals need the most time and which days are the most compressed, block actual review sessions into your calendar — not "study for chemistry" as a vague intention, but a specific block on a specific day. Spread review across multiple sessions per exam rather than one long cram block; spaced review generally holds up better under exam pressure than a single marathon session the night before.

## Step 5: Protect sleep and one thing you'd normally do anyway

It's tempting to cut sleep and every non-academic commitment during finals week, but exam performance depends on more than hours studied. Build in a normal sleep schedule and at least one thing — a workout, a meal with friends, whatever normally resets you — as a fixed, non-negotiable block on the calendar, not something that only happens if there's time left over.

## A realistic finals week

Here's what steps 1 through 3 look like applied to an actual schedule, rather than in the abstract. Say finals land like this:

- **Monday:** Biology at 9am
- **Tuesday:** no exams
- **Wednesday:** Calculus at 9am and History at 2pm
- **Thursday:** Spanish at 9am

Step 3 flags the obvious problem right away: Wednesday is a double-final day, and it's a bad day to discover that for the first time. Whatever review is left for either Wednesday exam has to happen before Wednesday, because there's no gap that day to add more study time between them — the night before Calculus is also the morning before History. In practice, that pushes most of the real review work for both Wednesday finals into the weekend and into Monday and Tuesday, on top of whatever Monday's Biology final still needs by then.

That's where steps 1 and 2 reshape the plan further. Suppose Biology, worth 20% of the course grade, currently sits at a comfortable 92 percent — a strong final grade in that course is already close to locked in barring a genuinely bad exam. Calculus, also worth 20%, is a much shakier 78 percent, borderline enough that a mediocre final could pull the course grade down a full letter grade. Even though Biology comes first on the calendar, the hours should skew toward Calculus: chronological order and actual need for study time are not the same thing, and treating "what's first" as "what's most urgent" is exactly the mistake steps 1 and 2 exist to catch.

Tuesday's open day, in this schedule, is the release valve — the obvious place to put the bulk of the Calculus and History review that can't happen the night before, precisely because Wednesday is already full. Thursday's Spanish final, by contrast, gets to keep a more normal lead-up, since nothing else that week is competing for the days before it.

## Use active recall and spaced practice during review sessions

Step 4 says to put review sessions on the calendar; it doesn't say what to actually do during them, and that choice matters almost as much as the number of hours scheduled. Rereading a textbook chapter or highlighting notes feels productive, but it mostly tests whether the material looks familiar when it's sitting right in front of you — which is a different skill than reconstructing it from memory under exam conditions, with no notes and a clock running.

Active recall closes that gap: covering the notes and trying to solve a practice problem, write out a definition, or explain a concept out loud as if teaching it, then checking the result against the source afterward. Research on learning generally finds that the effort of retrieving an answer, even imperfectly, builds a stronger and more durable memory than passively reviewing the same material again — a flashcard you struggle to answer is doing more for retention than one you read and instantly recognize.

Spacing works in a similar way. For the same total number of hours, retention generally comes out better when review is spread across several sessions on different days than when it's compressed into one long session the night before. Each return to the material after a gap forces the same effortful retrieval that makes active recall work, rather than letting surface familiarity stand in for actually knowing it. That's the real reason step 4 calls for several specific review blocks per exam instead of one large block — the spacing is doing part of the work, not just the hours themselves.

Practically, this means a review session should look less like reading and more like a quiz: working practice problems from scratch, running through flashcards, or summarizing a topic from memory before checking notes to see what got missed. Applied to the schedule above, a Calculus session would mean closing the textbook and working problem sets cold, not re-reading worked examples; a History session would mean writing out an answer to a likely essay prompt from memory and only then checking it against notes; a Spanish session would mean producing vocabulary and verb conjugations aloud rather than reading a list and recognizing them as correct.

## Exam-day logistics

A few practical details are worth confirming a day ahead rather than the morning of. Many schools publish a separate master final exam schedule that overrides the class's regular meeting time and room, precisely so exams from different courses don't collide — that master schedule, not the syllabus written in the first week of the semester, is the one to trust once it's out. Some finals move to a different room than the one the class normally meets in, especially in courses that combine several sections into one shared exam block, so checking the room is as important as checking the date and time. Confirm what's actually allowed during the exam too: a calculator, a single-page notes sheet, a specific scratch-paper policy — these vary by professor even within the same department, and finding out at the door is the wrong time to learn the answer. Eat something substantial before a multi-hour exam block; running a long exam on an empty stomach is a self-inflicted disadvantage that has nothing to do with how well the material is actually known.

## The point isn't a stress-free week

None of this makes finals week effortless, and it isn't meant to. The goal is narrower: making sure the stress that shows up during finals week is proportional to how genuinely hard the material is, rather than amplified by something that could have been seen coming — an exam cluster that caught you by surprise, or a grade you didn't realize was borderline until the week it mattered most.
