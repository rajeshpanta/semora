Every syllabus contains the same core information: when the class meets, how your grade is calculated, and every assignment, exam, and reading you're responsible for. The problem is that it arrives as a wall of text in a PDF, not as a calendar you can actually plan around. Here's how to convert one into the other — by hand, and with a scanner that does it for you.

## Step 1: Read the whole syllabus once before entering anything

It's tempting to start copying dates the moment you open a syllabus PDF, but a first read-through matters. Grading policies, late-work rules, and exam formats are often explained once near the top and referenced only briefly later — if you miss that context, a date on its own can be misleading (a "project due" date might really be a proposal, with the real deliverable due weeks later).

This first pass is also when to note anything unusual about how the course runs: a professor who drops the lowest quiz grade, a participation policy tied to attendance, or a late-work policy that docks a fixed percentage per day. None of that shows up as a "date," but all of it changes how you should treat the dates once you have them.

## Step 2: List the recurring structure first

Before individual assignments, capture the things that repeat all semester:

- Class meeting days and times
- Office hours
- Semester start and end dates
- The grading scale or category weights (for example, homework 20%, midterms 30%, final 30%, participation 20%)

This recurring structure is what turns a list of due dates into an actual calendar — it's the difference between "essay due October 14" and knowing that essay is worth 15% of your grade in a class that meets Tuesday and Thursday at 11 a.m.

## Step 3: Extract every graded item and its due date

Go section by section and list every assignment, quiz, exam, project, and required reading, each with its specific due date. Syllabi vary in how due dates are formatted — some list a single date, others reference "the Friday before spring break" or a rolling schedule — so resolve each one to an actual calendar date as you go, rather than leaving it as a note to interpret later.

### Common formatting quirks that trip people up

A few patterns show up often enough to call out specifically:

- **"TBD" or "see course site."** Some syllabi deliberately leave exam dates or project deadlines unset at the start of the term, to be announced later. Flag these rather than skipping them, so you remember to check back — an item that's silently missing from your calendar is worse than one marked as pending.
- **Rolling or recurring deadlines.** "Reading response due every Friday by 11:59pm" is one line in a syllabus but represents 12–15 actual deadlines across a semester. These need to be expanded into individual calendar entries, not left as a single recurring note you have to remember to apply.
- **Recitation or lab sections with a separate schedule.** Large lecture courses sometimes hand out a second document — a lab manual or recitation schedule — with its own deadlines that don't appear in the main syllabus at all. If your course has a lab or discussion section, check whether it has its own separate schedule.
- **Dates relative to class sessions rather than calendar dates.** "Due at the start of class 14" only means something once you've mapped class sessions to actual dates, which requires knowing the meeting pattern and any skipped holidays.

## Step 4: Put it in a calendar, not just a list

A list of dates is easy to lose track of. Moving everything into an actual calendar — one that shows a week or month at a glance — is what makes it possible to see when three exams are about to land in the same week, before that week arrives. Include class meeting times too, not just deadlines, so free study blocks are visible alongside the graded work competing for them.

## Step 5: Repeat for every course, then look across all of them together

The real value shows up once every course is in the same calendar. A single syllabus rarely looks overwhelming on its own; four of them stacked into the same week sometimes does. Seeing that in advance is what makes it possible to start an assignment early instead of discovering the collision the night before.

## A worked mini-example

Say a syllabus for an introductory statistics course includes this passage:

> "Weekly problem sets are due every Wednesday at 5pm, starting week 2. There will be two midterms (weeks 6 and 11) and a cumulative final during finals week. Grading: problem sets 20%, midterms 20% each, final 40%."

Turning that into calendar entries means:

1. **Expanding the recurring problem set line** into individual Wednesday-at-5pm entries starting in week 2 and continuing through the last week of regular classes — not one generic "problem sets due weekly" note.
2. **Converting "week 6" and "week 11"** into actual calendar dates, based on the semester's start date and the class's meeting pattern.
3. **Flagging the final exam date as pending** if the syllabus doesn't give an exact date yet — finals schedules are often published separately, partway through the term.
4. **Recording the weights** (20/20/20/40) alongside the categories, not just the dates, since that's what makes grade tracking possible later.

That's four categories and roughly fifteen individual dates extracted from three sentences of syllabus text — which is exactly why doing this by hand for four or five courses at once gets tedious fast.

## Keeping the calendar accurate as the semester moves

A syllabus is a snapshot from the first week of class, and professors change dates — a midterm moves, a reading gets swapped, a deadline slips because of a snow day. Whatever system holds your calendar needs a way to handle that without you manually re-auditing every entry:

- If you're maintaining a spreadsheet or paper planner, cross out and rewrite the specific entry that changed, and note the date you made the change (so if there's ever a dispute about which version was current, you have a record).
- If your school uses an LMS like Canvas, changes to assignment due dates there usually update automatically for anyone synced to it — which is one real advantage of connecting a course to its LMS rather than relying purely on a one-time syllabus scan.
- Recheck any item you flagged as "TBD" periodically rather than assuming it'll surface itself — professors don't always send a dedicated announcement when they finally set a placeholder date.

## How this compares to just using a generic calendar app

A calendar app like the default one on your phone can hold every date above just fine once you've entered it — the limitation isn't storage, it's the labor of getting the syllabus into that format in the first place, and keeping it there as things change. A generic calendar also has no concept of grading weight, so "Essay 2" and "Final Exam" look identical in the calendar even though one is worth ten times more to your final grade.
